Suffering is universal. What we believe about it is not.

I study the quiet beliefs that shape how people meet hardship — whether suffering is shared or singular, whether it can enrich as well as diminish — and the hypo-egoic capacities that grow alongside them: self-compassion and intellectual humility. Across U.S., Korean, and Israeli samples, these beliefs and capacities predict resilience, compassion, and the recognition of common humanity, even across lines of active conflict.

I am a postdoctoral associate at Duke University and UNC-Chapel Hill, working with Barbara Fredrickson, and Co-Principal Investigator and Project Director of Reframing Suffering to Foster Common Humanity and Compassion, a $1.3M John Templeton Foundation project (2026–2029). I completed my PhD at Duke in 2026, advised by Rick Hoyle and Mark Leary, following an earlier career as a biostatistician at the UNC School of Medicine.

Research

Hypo-egoic functioning

My dissertation (Duke, 2026) brought these strands together under a single question: what happens when the self quiets down? Hypo-egoic states — in which self-concern recedes rather than being defended or inflated — appear across self-compassion, intellectual humility, and the belief that suffering is shared. Working with U.S., Korean, and Israeli samples, I found that these self-processes travel together and predict a common set of outcomes: resilience under stress, compassion that extends past one's own group, and a sense of common humanity. The three lines below each develop one facet of that program.

Beliefs about suffering

Across five studies, viewing suffering as universal and potentially enriching — rather than only depressing — predicted better psychological adjustment, greater compassion, and, among Israeli adults surveyed during active conflict, recognition of outgroup suffering and support for peaceful resolution. Brief reframing shifted these beliefs experimentally. The flagship paper appears open access in Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin.

Self-compassion

A four-year longitudinal study shows stress and self-compassion working in synergy to build resilience — hardship itself becomes generative when met with self-kindness. With collaborators at UNC, I study self-compassion where the stakes are highest: an open trial reducing suicidal ideation in transgender adolescents, a randomized trial of Mindful Self-Compassion for Teens with subsyndromal depression, and programs for caregivers of children with disabilities. I have also written on self-compassion for general audiences in Korean.

Intellectual humility

Recognizing the limits of one's own knowledge has consequences beyond the epistemic: our work links intellectual humility to support for peaceful resolution of intergroup conflict (Journal of Applied Social Psychology) and to responsiveness to public health guidance (Personality and Individual Differences).

Methodologically, I work in scale development and psychometrics, structural equation modeling, survival analysis, hierarchical models, and machine learning based methods — a toolkit carried over from clinical biostatistics, where my collaborations span trials published in The Lancet Psychiatry and BMJ.
